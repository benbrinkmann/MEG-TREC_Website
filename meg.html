What Is MEG?

Magnetoencephalography (MEG) measures the magnetic fields produced by electrical activity in the brain. Sensors placed just outside the head record these fields with millisecond temporal resolution, and mathematical models are then used to estimate where in the brain the activity arose. MEG is completely noninvasive: it emits nothing and simply listens to the brain's own magnetic signals.1,2

The first recording of the brain's magnetic field was reported by David Cohen in 1968 using an induction coil magnetometer.3 In 1972 he recorded the alpha rhythm with a superconducting quantum interference device (SQUID) inside a magnetically shielded room, the basic arrangement that most clinical systems still use.4 Modern whole head systems contain a few hundred sensors arranged in a helmet that covers the scalp.

In one sentence: MEG offers the temporal resolution of EEG with spatial information that is less distorted by the skull and scalp, at the cost of more demanding instrumentation.

Origin of the Signal

The MEG signal arises mainly from postsynaptic currents flowing within the apical dendrites of cortical pyramidal neurons. These cells are aligned in parallel, perpendicular to the cortical surface, so when many of them receive synchronous synaptic input their intracellular currents (the "primary currents") add together. A useful approximation for such a patch of cortex is an equivalent current dipole.1

A measurable MEG signal requires synchronous activity across a cortical patch that typically contains on the order of tens of thousands of neurons.5 Action potentials contribute relatively little because they are brief and their current pattern resembles a quadrupole, whose field falls off rapidly with distance.

Orientation matters

In a spherically symmetric conductor, a dipole that points radially (directly toward the sensors) produces no magnetic field outside the head.6 MEG is therefore most sensitive to tangential sources, such as those on the walls of sulci, and relatively insensitive to purely radial sources at the crowns of gyri. The real head is not a perfect sphere, so this is a matter of degree rather than an absolute rule. EEG, in contrast, detects both orientations, which is one reason the two methods are complementary.

Field Strength and Shielding

Brain magnetic fields are extremely small, in the range of tens to a few thousand femtotesla (1 fT = 10−15 tesla). The Earth's static field is roughly a billion times stronger, and the fluctuating fields from traffic, elevators, and power lines are also much larger than the brain signal.1 Recording MEG therefore depends on several layers of noise reduction:

  • Magnetically shielded rooms built from layers of high permeability alloy (mu metal) and aluminum attenuate external fields.
  • Gradiometer sensors respond to differences in field over a short distance, which suppresses distant noise sources while preserving nearby brain signals.
  • Reference sensors and software methods such as signal space separation remove residual interference after recording (see Preprocessing).

Instrumentation

SQUID sensors

Conventional MEG systems detect magnetic fields with SQUIDs, superconducting circuits that convert very small changes in magnetic flux into a measurable voltage. Each SQUID is coupled to a pickup coil (the "flux transformer") whose geometry determines what the sensor measures. SQUIDs must be kept superconducting at about 4 kelvin, so the sensors sit in a helmet shaped dewar filled with liquid helium, typically 2 to 3 cm from the scalp.1,2

Pickup coil geometries

  • Magnetometers use a single loop and measure the field component through it. They are sensitive to deep sources and also to distant noise.
  • Axial gradiometers use two coaxial loops wound in opposite directions, so that uniform fields cancel and fields from nearby sources remain.
  • Planar gradiometers use a figure eight coil that measures the field gradient along the scalp. They give their largest signal directly over a source and are less sensitive to deep sources.

Commercial systems differ in how they combine these. For example, MEGIN (formerly Elekta Neuromag) systems contain 306 channels at 102 locations, each with one magnetometer and two orthogonal planar gradiometers, while CTF systems use roughly 275 axial gradiometers.

Optically pumped magnetometers

Optically pumped magnetometers (OPMs) measure magnetic fields using the response of a heated alkali vapor to laser light. They do not require cryogenic cooling, so they can be mounted directly on the scalp in a wearable helmet. Placing sensors closer to the head increases signal amplitude and allows recording while the participant moves.7 OPMs operate near zero background field, so they are typically used in shielded rooms with additional field nulling coils. OPM systems are an active area of development and are not yet as widely used clinically as SQUID systems.

Recording a Study

A typical clinical or research MEG session includes the following steps:8,9

  • Screening for metal on or in the body (dental work, implants, clothing, makeup) that may create magnetic artifacts.
  • Head digitization. Anatomical landmarks (the nasion and the two preauricular points), the positions of head position indicator (HPI) coils, and several hundred points on the scalp are recorded with a 3D digitizer. These are later used to align the MEG data with the patient's MRI.
  • Head position tracking. Small currents in the HPI coils let the system locate the head within the helmet, at the start of a run or continuously, so that head movement can be measured and compensated.
  • Recording. Spontaneous activity is recorded at rest and, for epilepsy evaluations, often during drowsiness and sleep to capture interictal discharges. Simultaneous EEG and electrocardiography are commonly recorded. Evoked response protocols (somatosensory, auditory, visual, motor, and language tasks) are added for functional mapping.

Noise Reduction and Preprocessing

Before source analysis, data are cleaned of interference and physiological artifacts. Common steps include:

  • Signal space separation (SSS) and its temporal extension (tSSS). These methods use the geometry of the sensor array to separate fields originating inside the sensor helmet from those originating outside it, and can also correct for head movement.10
  • Signal space projection (SSP) and independent component analysis (ICA) to remove cardiac, eye blink, and other stereotyped artifacts.
  • Filtering to the frequency band of interest, and marking or rejecting segments with movement or muscle artifact.

Good practice recommendations for acquisition, analysis, and reporting are summarized by Gross and colleagues.9

Source Modeling

Estimating brain activity from MEG involves two related problems.

The forward problem

The forward model predicts the field each sensor would measure for a source at a given location and orientation. It requires the sensor positions, the coregistration between MEG and MRI, and a model of the head. Because magnetic fields are less affected than electric potentials by the conductivity of the skull and scalp, relatively simple head models (a single sphere or overlapping local spheres) often work well for MEG. Boundary element models built from the patient's MRI provide a more realistic geometry.1,2

The inverse problem

The inverse problem estimates the sources that produced the measured fields. It has no unique solution: different combinations of sources inside the head can produce the same field outside it, a result first shown by Helmholtz in the nineteenth century. Each inverse method therefore adds assumptions to arrive at a single answer, and understanding those assumptions is essential when interpreting results.

ApproachMain assumptionTypical uses
Equivalent current dipole (ECD) Activity is focal and can be represented by one or a few dipoles. Interictal epileptiform discharges; early evoked responses. The standard clinical method for spike localization.8
Distributed minimum norm methods (MNE, dSPM, sLORETA) Activity is spread across many cortical locations; the solution with the least overall current is preferred, with various normalizations. Spatially extended activity; group studies; time courses of cortical activity.11
Beamformers (LCMV, DICS, SAM) Activity at different locations is not strongly correlated over time. Oscillatory activity; ictal and interictal analyses; connectivity.12
Maximum entropy on the mean (MEM) Activity is organized in cortical parcels that are either active or inactive. Estimating the spatial extent of sources, including epileptic generators.

Clinical Applications

Presurgical evaluation of epilepsy

The most common clinical use of MEG is localizing interictal epileptiform discharges in patients with drug resistant focal epilepsy who are being considered for surgery. Dipoles fitted to individual spikes are displayed on the patient's MRI, and a tight cluster of dipoles with consistent orientation supports a focal generator. MEG findings can direct a second look at an MRI that was initially read as normal, help generate hypotheses for intracranial electrode placement such as stereo EEG, and add confidence when concordant with other tests.8 Seizures are occasionally captured during MEG and can also be analyzed.

Presurgical functional mapping

MEG can identify eloquent cortex before resection of tumors, vascular lesions, or epileptic foci, reducing the risk of postoperative deficits. Established protocols map somatosensory cortex with median nerve or tactile stimulation, auditory cortex with tones, visual cortex with patterned stimuli, and motor cortex with voluntary movement. Language tasks can help establish hemispheric dominance and localize receptive language areas, often as a complement to other methods such as functional MRI or the intracarotid amobarbital procedure.

Clinical guidelines. The American Clinical Magnetoencephalography Society (ACMEGS) has published clinical practice guidelines for recording and analyzing spontaneous activity and for presurgical functional mapping.8

Research Applications

Because MEG tracks neural activity directly and with high temporal resolution, it is widely used to study brain oscillations and how brain regions interact. Areas represented at MEG-TREC meetings include:

  • Functional connectivity and networks, including network measures as potential biomarkers.
  • Neurodevelopment, where wearable OPM systems may allow recording in young children who cannot stay still.
  • Aging and neurodegenerative disease, where network changes may precede clinical symptoms.
  • Multimodal integration of MEG with EEG, structural MRI, and functional imaging.
  • Brain computer interfaces that decode brain activity in real time.

Analysis Software

In addition to the clinical software supplied by MEG manufacturers, several open source packages are widely used. Each offers tutorials with sample data:

  • Brainstorm: a graphical application (MATLAB based, with a compiled version) for MEG, EEG, and intracranial EEG analysis.
  • MNE-Python: a Python package covering preprocessing, source estimation, and statistics.
  • FieldTrip: a MATLAB toolbox for MEG, EEG, and invasive electrophysiology.
  • SPM: statistical parametric mapping, including MEG and EEG source reconstruction and dynamic causal modeling.
